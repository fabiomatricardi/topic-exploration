# Does Your Substack Pass the 15-Second Napkin Test?

> Source: https://escapethecubicle.substack.com/p/does-your-substack-pass-the-15-second
A friend of mine is in business school right now.

In one of his classes, everyone has to create a fictitious business and write a two-sentence plan on the back of a napkin.

Then they have to stand in front of the class and explain the idea as simply as possible.

*No long presentation or complicated language. No hiding behind 30 PowerPoint slides.*

Just a napkin and a clear explanation.

> That got me thinking:
>
>
>
> ***Would my Substack pass the 15-second napkin test?***

I’ve been writing here for more than two years now, but the first few months were rocky.

I didn’t really know who I was writing for. I had several things I wanted to talk about, but there wasn’t a clear thread connecting them.

If someone had asked me what my Substack was about during those first few months, my answer probably would’ve changed depending on the day…

***And if I couldn’t clearly explain who my Substack was for, how could I expect a new reader to understand?***

It wasn’t until I slowed down and got specific about my story, my reader, and what I wanted to help them accomplish that things began to change.

Once I became clearer, my writing became clearer too—and the right people finally had a reason to subscribe.

## **So, does your Substack pass the test?**

Grab a napkin (or just open your phone’s Notes app) and answer these three questions:

1. **Who do you write for?**
2. **What is your Substack about?**
3. **What problem does your Substack help readers solve?**

Now turn those answers into one or two clear sentences.

Then imagine a stranger asks what your Substack is about.

***Can you explain it in 15 seconds?***

Not with a five-minute story about everything you’ve experienced. Not with a list of six different topics.

*And not with vague language about writing on whatever inspires you that week.*

You should be able to explain it simply enough that the other person immediately understands who it’s for and why they might want to subscribe.

For example, I could say:

> I help new Substack writers grow their audience and monetize it through clearer positioning, a consistent Notes strategy, and simple digital products.

That tells someone who I write for, what I write about, and what I help them accomplish.

Your sentence doesn’t need to sound clever.

***It needs to be clear.***

## **This is where many good writers get stuck**

A new reader might find one of your Posts, enjoy it, and even leave a like or comment.

Then they visit your profile and try to understand what the larger Substack is about.

If your description is vague, your topics feel disconnected, or they can’t tell what they’ll receive next, they may leave without subscribing.

*That doesn’t mean the post wasn’t good.*

It means you gave them a reason to read today, but not a clear reason to subscribe for tomorrow.

***Most Substacks don’t have a writing problem. They have a “Why should I subscribe?” problem.***

And I understand why this is difficult.

When you’re starting, you may still be finding your voice. You don’t want to box yourself into one narrow subject, and you may not know exactly where your writing will eventually take you.

*I felt the same way.*

But clarity doesn’t mean you can never explore a related idea or allow your Substack to evolve.

It simply means there’s a recognizable thread connecting what you write, who you write it for, and why it matters to them.
